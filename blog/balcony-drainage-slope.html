Finding Out Why Water Pooled on My Balcony

By Raghul Babu · Published 20 October 2026

Every time it rained, a puddle formed right in front of our balcony door and stayed for a day. A balcony is supposed to slope gently away from the building toward the drain. I wanted to know whether ours did, and if not, where it went wrong, before talking to the landlord. Here's how I mapped it with my phone, and where the phone wasn't enough on its own.

How much slope is normal?

Balconies and terraces are usually built with a fall of around 1–2% toward the drain. That is very gentle: 1% is about 0.6° and 2% is about 1.1°. A phone inclinometer that shows whole degrees will read 0° or 1° for most of a correctly built balcony. So I used the phone to find the direction of the slope at each spot, and a spirit level to measure how big the problem was.

What you need

  • A smartphone
  • A long, straight board or aluminium straight edge, 1–1.5 m long
  • Chalk or masking tape to mark spots
  • A spirit level and a tape measure for the final measurement
  • A sketch of the balcony to write readings on

How I mapped it

  1. I swept the balcony and marked a rough grid of spots about a metre apart with chalk.
  2. At each spot, I laid the board pointing toward the drain and put the phone on top with its top pointing at the drain. A positive or negative Pitch (X) then tells you whether that spot runs toward the drain or away from it. I noted the sign on the sketch.
  3. Most spots read 1° toward the drain. The metre in front of the door read 1° the wrong way, toward the door.
  4. To put a number on it, I laid the spirit level on the board in that spot and lifted the low end until the bubble was centred, then measured the gap. It was about 12 mm over 1.2 m, a 1% slope back toward the door.

What it turned out to be

The sketch made the problem obvious: the tiles near the door had settled, creating a shallow dip below the door threshold. The drain outlet was also half blocked with leaves, which made everything drain slower. I cleared the outlet and gave the landlord the sketch with the readings on it. A sketch with numbers was much easier to discuss than "there's a puddle".

TipMark your spots and write every reading on a sketch as you go, including which way it leans. A pattern you can see on paper is easy to miss when you're crouched over one tile at a time.
